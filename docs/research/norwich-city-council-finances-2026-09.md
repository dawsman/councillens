# Norwich City Council: how it budgets, and whether it lives within its means

Research note for CouncilLens, **version 2**. Covers financial years 2019/20 to 2026/27, with a
longer run where the story needs it. This version follows an adversarial verification of v1 against
the saved documents; every correction is listed in section 7. Every figure traces to a document saved in this folder; the file is named
beside it. Public sources only, all of them the council's own papers or national statistics.

**Two things to fix in your head before any of the numbers.**

Norwich City Council is a district council. It sends you the council tax bill but keeps about 13p
of every pound: 73p goes to Norfolk County Council and 14p to the Police and Crime Commissioner
(`raw/news_fresh-approach-setting-our-budget-2026-27.html`, 4 December 2025). Its own net revenue
budget is roughly £21–24m. Nothing here tells you anything about social care, schools or roads,
which are the county's.

It also keeps two separate pots. The **General Fund** pays for bins, parks, planning, benefits
administration, markets and the rest. The **Housing Revenue Account** pays for about 14,000
council homes, and is ring-fenced by law: rent money can only be spent on housing, and General
Fund money cannot be used to prop it up. I have kept them apart throughout. Mixing them is the
commonest way to get a council's finances wrong.

## 1. Five things worth knowing

**1. For three years running the council's service departments have overspent, and each year the
gap was closed by the central pot rather than by the services.**
Norwich has closed every one of the last five years with a General Fund underspend, which reads as
reassuring until you look at where it comes from. Since 2023/24 the service directorates have gone
over budget every year, and every year "Corporate Financing" — the central line that carries debt
costs, investment interest and movements to and from reserves — has come in far enough under to
cover them. In 2023/24 services were £2.011m over and the year still ended £4.050m in hand, on a
£6.061m Corporate Financing underspend. In 2024/25 services were £0.118m over, which on a roughly
£65m base is 0.18% and three of the four directorates actually underspent; that year is a rounding
difference, not a run of failure. In 2025/26 services were £2.282m over against a £2.992m Corporate
Financing underspend.

What filled the gap is worth taking from the council's own words rather than inferring. Its list of
significant variances for 2023/24 puts investment interest fourth: £2.8m came from "a budgeted
drawdown of reserves that wasn't required due to the improved budget position", £1.950m from "a
revised approach to the minimum revenue position calculation" approved by Council on professional
advice, then £1.458m from interest on day-to-day cash, then £0.568m of Funding Guarantee grant. For
2024/25 the council names £0.809m of interest alongside underspends in Resources (£1.116m) and
Communities and Housing (£0.706m). For 2025/26 it names £1.286m of interest as "contributing to the
overall underspend of £2.992m within Corporate Financing".

So interest is a named and growing component — £1.458m, £0.809m, £1.286m — and the council has
built it into the plan: its February 2025 budget report says high cash balances and high rates
delivered "over £4.5m in investment returns" and that "we have been able to reflect those benefits
in the Medium-Term Financial Strategy" (para 18). Bank Rate is not something a district council
controls, and its own treasury adviser's November 2025 forecast points downwards. One caution for
whoever writes this up: para 16 of the same report mentions "around £5m per annum in investment
returns" from the **asset base** — commercial property. That is different money from treasury
interest. Do not merge the two figures. Sources: Narrative Report, "Financial Performance", in each
Statement of Accounts — `raw/soa/SOA-2023-24.pdf` pp.8–9, `raw/soa/SOA-2024-25.pdf` p.12,
`raw/soa/SOA-2025-26-draft.pdf` p.14; `raw/budget/BudgetCouncil-2025.pdf` para 18;
`raw/tms/TMS-2026-27.pdf` Appendix C.
https://www.norwich.gov.uk/your-council-explained/transparency-and-accountability/statement-accounts

**2. Day-to-day spending has come in under budget five years running, by between 1% and 6%, with
the largest miss in 2023/24. The building programme has not come near plan once in those five
years.**
Day-to-day spending has come in under budget in each of the last five years, by between £0.710m and
£4.050m. As a share of the budget the council reports misses of 2.18% (2021/22), 2.76% (2022/23),
6.23% (2023/24), 1.18% (2024/25) and 1.03% (2025/26). Two of those five are within about 1%; the
2023/24 figure is the largest of the run and belongs in any chart. The percentages are also not
strictly comparable, because the council changed the figure it divides by partway through: the
2021/22 percentage is struck against a roughly £109m gross expenditure budget, the later ones
against a roughly £65–69m gross General Fund expenditure budget. Capital is a different animal. Against a
five-year capital budget of £342.9m the council actually spent £218.6m — 64%. The best year was
2024/25 at 79%, the worst 2021/22 at 50%, and 2025/26 came in at 60% with £38.3m of approved work
not done. Cumulatively £124.4m of approved capital spending did not happen between 2021/22 and
2025/26. Much of that is ordinary construction slippage and the money is not lost, but the
rolling-forward is why the in-year capital budget keeps inflating: councillors approved a
£67.453m programme in February 2025, and by the year-end accounts the 2025/26 capital budget
stood at £94.768m. Sources: capital tables in the Narrative Report of each Statement of Accounts,
`raw/soa/SOA-2021-22.pdf` p.10 through `raw/soa/SOA-2025-26-draft.pdf` p.15;
`raw/budget/BudgetCouncil-2025.pdf` para 25.

**3. The council's housing company was wound up, and the auditors say nobody had ever been
watching it properly.**
Lion Homes (Norwich) Ltd — set up as Norwich Regeneration Ltd in 2015 to build homes for sale and
rent and to earn the council an income — went into members' voluntary liquidation in 2025. On
31 March 2025 it could not repay £6.15m of loans that had fallen due, and the council "impaired
the total amount of the loans to nil"; the shareholding was written down from £3.424m to £2.825m.
The company's own 2023/24 accounts carry a qualified audit opinion because of an ongoing
independent investigation arising from a whistleblowing complaint. Ernst & Young reported a
significant weakness in the council's value-for-money arrangements, in unusually blunt language:
"Based on the evidence available, there is no indication that adequate oversight arrangements for
subsidiaries have ever existed, dating back to the subsidiary's incorporation in November 2015",
and the absence of oversight "exposed the Council - and ultimately local taxpayers - to an
increased risk of financial loss". Quote that first sentence whole. Starting it at "there is no
indication" turns a hedged auditor's finding into a flat assertion, and on a site whose premise is
traceable quotation that matters. In fairness to the council, the wind-up was **solvent**:
Cabinet's July 2025 report records that creditors will be paid in full, "the Council will retain
the assets of the company", funds had already been set aside against the risk, and no extra funding
was needed. The final cost will not be
known until the liquidation completes. Sources: `raw/soa/AuditCert-2023-24.pdf`;
`raw/soa/SOA-2024-25.pdf` notes 10, 19, 35 and Group Accounts;
`raw/cmis/2025-07-09_Cabinet_lion-homes.pdf` paras 8, 12–15; `raw/soa/AGS-2025-26.pdf` pp.14–15.
https://www.norwich.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2026-06/AuditCertificateSOA2023-24.pdf

**4. Commercial property used to bring in about a pound in six of the council's core funding. It
now brings in about one in twenty-five.**
The council publishes a prudential indicator called "net commercial income as a percentage of net
revenue stream" — how much of its core money comes from renting commercial buildings and lending
to third parties. In 2021/22 that was 16%. In 2022/23, 18%. By 2024/25 it was 4%, with 3–4%
forecast for the rest of the decade. In cash, the council's own "net commercial income" line fell
from £3.531m to £1.219m.

Two cautions before anyone draws that as a smooth decline. The council added a notional £1.755m of
borrowing cost for properties bought between 2017 and 2020 into the calculation, so the later years
are struck on a slightly harsher basis than the earlier ones. And the figure it divides by — the
net revenue stream — grew from £21.416m to £29.032m over the same period, up more than a third. The
council's own forward estimates never matched the outturn either: in February 2023 it forecast
14–15% through 2024/25 and the actual came in at 4%.

The portfolio has shrunk from £119.4m at March 2022 to £96.2m at March 2026, and the largest single
step was not a write-down: the council **sold** £25.888m of it in 2022/23, in a year when
revaluation actually added £5.062m. Revaluations since have gone both ways — down £5.896m in
2023/24, down £1.342m in 2024/25, up £4.075m in 2025/26 — and are reversed out in the accounts so
they never touch the council tax requirement. The shrinking net income does. The council
still holds just under £100m of investment property, some of it a long way from Norfolk — a gym
in Ramsgate, Oakland International in Corby, offices in Cambridge and Milton Keynes. It has been
retreating from this strategy rather than extending it: the Norwich Airport Industrial Estate was
sold after a Cabinet decision in July 2021, and PWLB no longer lends to councils buying property
for yield. Sources: `raw/budget/BudgetCouncil-2023.pdf` Table 6.3; the equivalent tables in
`raw/tms/TMS-2024-25.pdf`, `TMS-2025-26.pdf`, `TMS-2026-27.pdf` §3;
`raw/soa/SOA-2024-25.pdf` note 16; `raw/ta_commercial-property-investments.html`.

**5. For six years running at least half of consultation respondents backed the council tax rise.
In 2026/27, for the first time, more opposed it than supported it, and the rise went through
unchanged.**
The council consults on the rise each year and publishes the result in the budget report. Seven of
those results are in the saved papers, an unbroken run from 2020/21, and it turns over in the most
recent year (I did not retrieve a headline figure for the 2019/20 consultation):

| Consulted for | Result the council published | Rise adopted |
|---|---|---|
| 2020/21 | 58% agree or strongly agree | 1.99% |
| 2021/22 | 58% agree or strongly agree | 1.99% |
| 2022/23 | 50% agree or strongly agree | 1.99% |
| 2023/24 | 50% support, 38% oppose (2,654 responses) | 2.99% |
| 2024/25 | 53% support, 39% oppose | 2.99% |
| 2025/26 | 50% in favour, 30% against (2,176 responses) | 2.99% |
| **2026/27** | **28% support, 48% oppose, 25% neutral (899 responses)** | **2.99%** |

For 2026/27, asked "how much do you support or oppose increasing the city's share of council tax by
2.99%?", 430 of 899 respondents opposed or strongly opposed and 248 supported — about 1.7 to one,
with 221 neutral. Council approved 2.99% on 24 February 2026, taking Band D to £315.26.

The honest reading is not that a consultation was ignored four years running. In six of the seven
years at least half of respondents supported the rise — 58%, 58%, 53% in three of them and exactly
50% in the other three — and in every year where the council published an opposing figure, support
outnumbered opposition. So there was no gap between the result and the decision.

What changed in 2026/27 is that the balance flipped and the figure did not move. A consultation is
also not a vote: the council has a legal duty to set a balanced budget, and 2.99%
is the most it can charge without a referendum. I could not find a year in which a consultation
result changed a published figure — but I also could not find a year before 2026/27 in which it
would have had to.

The same 2026/27 survey found 38% of respondents unhappy or very unhappy with how the council
delivers services against 27% happy, and the council published that unflattering result in full.
Responses fell to 899 from a post-2019 average of 1,361, and the council states that "no specific
factors have been identified to explain the variation in response rate". Sources:
`raw/cmis/2026-02-11_Cabinet_section-2-general-fund-mtfs-and-2026-2027-budget.pdf` Appendix 2(F),
questions 4 and 14; `raw/cmis/2025-02-05_Cabinet_section-2-general-fund-mtfs-and-2025-2026-budget.pdf`
App 2(F); `raw/cmis/2024-02-07_Cabinet_section-2-general-fund-mtfs-and-2024-2025-budget.pdf`
App 2(E); `raw/budget/BudgetCouncil-2023.pdf` para 2.9; `BudgetCouncil-2022.pdf` para 2.9;
`BudgetCouncil-2021.pdf` para 2.9; `BudgetCouncil-2020.pdf` para 2.9;
`raw/news_cuts-frontline-services-avoided-after-citys-share-council-tax-agreed.html`.

Something that did not make the five, but nearly did: the General Fund working balance has sat at
exactly £8.249m at 31 March in 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026 — four consecutive year-ends, which is
three years without a draw-down — while every medium-term forecast in that period predicted a gap.
See section 3.3.

## 2. How the budget is built — the annual cycle in plain English

The council publishes its own guide, which is a good sign in itself, and it links every budget
report back to February 2012:
https://www.norwich.gov.uk/your-council-explained/transparency-and-accountability/norwich-city-council-budget-2026-27-what-you

**Summer to autumn.** Officers update the Medium Term Financial Strategy, a rolling four- or
five-year forecast of what services will cost against the money expected. The gap becomes next
year's savings target. An update goes to Cabinet in July or November.

**Early December.** In most years Cabinet publishes a provisional budget and MTFS — 14 December
2022, 13 December 2023, 11 December 2024. For 2026/27 the council changed the process: the HRA
business plan and budget went to Cabinet on 3 December 2025 and the consultation launched the next
day, with the full General Fund budget going straight to Cabinet on 11 February 2026.

**December to mid-January.** Public consultation on Get Talking Norwich, running about five weeks.
The 2026/27 one opened on 4 December 2025 and closed on 18 January 2026, promoted by text alert
to 120,000 residents, email, social media and partner networks. There is a statutory duty to
consult business ratepayers (s65, Local Government Finance Act 1992). Consulting residents is the
council's own choice.

**Mid-January.** Tenant Involvement Panel representatives are consulted separately on the
proposed council house rent rise. This is the HRA side. Their view is minuted: in December 2023
they raised concerns about a 7.7% increase but endorsed it.

**Early February.** Budget Scrutiny Committee spends a session on the draft. Cabinet meets days
later and formally adopts its recommendations. This is the stage that demonstrably changes
things — see section 4.

**Mid-to-late February.** All 39 councillors vote at Budget Council on the General Fund budget,
the council tax level, the HRA budget, the capital programme, the Treasury Management Strategy
and the reserves strategy. Political groups can table costed amendments; for 2026/27 the Labour
group added £313,000 of items funded from contingency (enforcement against fly-tipping and
graffiti, communal-area improvements, community clean-ups) and the Green and Liberal Democrat
groups tabled their own, including a £65,000 ward-level community cohesion fund and £45,000 for
tree planting at a stated £459 per tree. The Chief Finance Officer must also give a statutory
statement under s25 of the Local Government Act 2003 on whether the estimates are robust and the
reserves adequate, and Council has to have regard to it.
Sources: `raw/cmis/2026-02-24_Council_updated-annex-c-labour-group-and-opposition-proposed-budget-amendments-v3.pdf`;
`raw/budget/BudgetCouncil-2023.pdf` §7.

**1 April.** The budget starts.

**June or July.** The outturn report goes to Cabinet: what was actually spent against budget,
service by service. Recent ones: 14 June 2023, 17 July 2024, 9 July 2025, 10 June 2026.

**Through the year.** Quarterly budget monitoring to Cabinet, a mid-year treasury review, and a
separate Treasury Management Committee that meets quarterly.

Two changes for 2026/27 worth flagging. The council has introduced what it calls a **live
budget**, reviewed during the year rather than only at year-end. And it has created a ring-fenced
**Readiness Fund** with a £1m contingency for Local Government Reorganisation and in-year
inflation — LGR being the national programme that may abolish this council and fold it into a
larger unitary authority. Every forecast beyond 2026/27 in these documents carries that caveat,
put there by the council itself.

£0.260m. A year later that plan was replaced by a flat line, and on the restated figures the
balance has not moved across four consecutive year-ends — three full years without a draw-down.
Meanwhile the MTFS predicted a gap every year that arrived smaller than forecast: the February
2019 plan put the 2023/24 gap at £10.405m, and when 2023/24 came the council balanced it with
£5.5m of savings and no reserve draw at all.

## 4. Is the council living within its means?

"Fiscally responsible" is a judgement, so here is the evidence each way and, more usefully, what
the people whose statutory job it is to judge have actually written down.

### The evidence for

- **Balanced every year without touching the working balance since 2022/23.** The last draw was
  £2.1m in 2022/23. The balance has sat at £8.249m ever since, against a prudent minimum of £5.5m
  set by the council's own finance officer. MHCLG's returns independently confirm nil
  appropriations from unallocated reserves in every year from 2023/24 to 2026/27.
- **Tight revenue control, with one wide year.** Five consecutive underspends. The last two came
  in within 1.2% of a £65–69m gross budget; 2023/24 missed by 6.23%, which is a large surplus
  rather than a large overspend but is still a big miss against plan.
- **Debt is cheap, and low against the council's own limits.** Nearly all external debt is PWLB —
  government lending at government rates. About two thirds is housing debt repaid from rents at
  roughly £15,000 per home. It is not, however, flat: on correctly aligned years the borrowing
  requirement has risen in each of the last three years, and the council plans about £42m of new
  external borrowing in 2026/27. See 3.7.
- **The external auditor has reported no weakness in financial sustainability in any year checked.**
  For 2021/22 and 2022/23 EY concluded: "the Council had proper arrangements in place… to plan and
  manage its resources to ensure that it can continue to deliver its services", with "no
  significant weakness identified". For 2023/24 and 2024/25 the weaknesses reported concern
  governance and financial reporting, and none is reported against financial sustainability. That
  is an absence of a finding rather than a positive conclusion, because I do not hold the full
  Audit Results Report for those years — see section 5. State it as the table states it.
- **EY recorded a clear improvement on the housing safety failure.** "Throughout 2022/23 the
  Council demonstrated significant progress in establishing arrangements which have led to the
  removal of the regulatory notice."
  (`raw/cmis/2024-07-17_Audit_external-audit-value-for-money-interim-report-2021-22-and-2022-23.pdf`)
- **No section 114 notice, no Public Interest Report, no government intervention.** The only
  mention of s114 in any document I read is the February 2025 report observing that other councils
  have issued them.
- **It has been retreating from commercial risk, not adding to it.** The airport industrial estate
  sold in 2021/22, the property portfolio broadly flat since 2023 after a £25.9m disposal in
  2022/23, PWLB lending for yield closed off, and
  the housing company wound up.
- **Candour in the papers.** The 2025/26 report volunteers that the parking income budget had been
  wrong. The 2026/27 consultation appendix publishes a result showing 38% of respondents unhappy
  with service delivery and 48% opposed to the tax rise, and states plainly that the drop in
  responses cannot be explained.

### The evidence against

- **The balancing item sits outside the services that are overspending, and partly outside the
  council's control.** Service overspends have been covered by Corporate Financing for three years.
  Investment interest is a named and growing part of that (£1.458m, £0.809m, £1.286m) and the
  council has written those returns into the medium-term plan, while its own treasury adviser
  forecasts Bank Rate falling. The other large 2023/24 contributors — an unneeded reserve drawdown
  and a change to the minimum revenue provision calculation — are one-offs by their nature.
- **Two significant weaknesses in value for money for 2024/25, one of them applied retrospectively
  to 2023/24.** First, oversight of subsidiaries. Second, and separately, **timely financial
  reporting**: EY told the council it needs to "re-assess roles, responsibilities and resource
  requirements for financial reporting" and to ensure sufficient audit evidence exists for
  transactions in the statements. Note that EY had found financial reporting arrangements
  *appropriate* in 2021/22 and 2022/23 — so this is a deterioration, and the accounts for 2024/25
  were signed by an **interim** section 151 officer.
- **A regulatory notice on council housing safety, since cleared.** After an internal review in
  June 2021 the council self-referred to the Regulator of Social Housing. In October 2021 the RSH
  issued a Regulatory Notice finding the council had breached the Home Standard with "the
  potential for serious detriment" to tenants and had no effective system for meeting its
  statutory health and safety duties. EY treated this as a significant weakness in 2021/22. A
  Compliance Improvement Plan and voluntary undertaking followed, and the RSH removed the notice
  in December 2023. Source: `raw/cmis/2024-07-17_Audit_external-audit-value-for-money-interim-report-2021-22-and-2022-23.pdf` pp.11–13.
- **Four consecutive years of disclaimed audit opinions** (2021/22 to 2024/25). This is
  overwhelmingly a national problem: the government's "backstop date" reset cleared a countrywide
  audit backlog by disclaiming thousands of authority-years. It is not evidence that Norwich's
  numbers are wrong. It does mean that for four years running nobody independently verified them.
  Sources: `SOA-2024-25.pdf` note 33; `AuditCert-2023-24.pdf`; `AuditCert-2024-25.pdf`.
- **The company failure was expensive, even solvent.** £6.15m of loans impaired to nil, the
  shareholding written down from £3.424m to £2.825m, and a £4m impairment against the loan book
  recognised as far back as 2019/20 (`BudgetCouncil-2021.pdf` §5.24). The council had approved a
  loan facility of up to £21m and equity of up to £6.2m for that company (§5.21). Total loss
  allowance against the council's financial assets was £12.148m at 1 April 2024 and £9.901m at
  31 March 2026. Against that: the assets come back to the council and creditors are paid.
- **Audit fees have jumped, though not in one direction.** £261k in 2023/24, £322k in 2024/25,
  against a PSAA scale fee of £230k — the council recognised an extra £55k for "ongoing additional
  professional and regulatory requirements relating to the audit process". The same note also
  records that "the estimated audit fees for these years have been reviewed and the accrual for
  unbilled fees reduced by £86k" (`SOA-2024-25.pdf` note 33).
- **Capital plans are consistently undeliverable at the scale approved.** 64% delivery over five
  years is not a control failure in the dangerous sense, but it does mean the published capital
  programme is not a reliable statement of what will actually be built next year.

### What residents can influence, and the honest answer about the consultation

The consultation is genuine and reasonably well run: five weeks, promoted by text to 120,000
residents, results published in full as an appendix to the budget report with demographic and
ward-level breakdowns. Response numbers: 2,654 for 2023/24, 2,176 for 2025/26, 899 for 2026/27
against a post-2019 average of 1,361.

In every year from 2019/20 to 2026/27 the council proposed a council tax rise and adopted exactly
that rise, and I could not find a case where a consultation changed a published number. That is a
weaker finding than it looks, and v1 overstated it. In six of the seven years with a published
result (2020/21 through 2025/26) at least half of respondents supported the rise — three years at
58%, 58% and 53%, three at exactly 50% — and support outnumbered opposition wherever both were
published. So there was no gap between what respondents said and what the council did. The consultation cannot be shown to
have been ignored in those years because it cannot be shown to have pointed anywhere else.

2026/27 is the first year in the run where that is not true: 48% opposed against 28% in support,
and the 2.99% went through unchanged. That single year is the fair version of this finding. The
counterweight still holds: the council has a legal duty to set a balanced budget, 2.99% is the
ceiling before a referendum, and the survey also asks about priorities and approach, where any
influence would be harder to trace either way. Full year-by-year results are in section 1,
finding 5.

**Budget Scrutiny Committee and Budget Council are where things demonstrably change.** Every
February scrutiny meets a week before Cabinet, and Cabinet formally adopts its recommendations
into the budget that Council then votes on. Documented examples:

- **February 2022** (scrutiny 3 Feb): a continued political commitment to renewable energy
  investment, and a fabric-first standard for heating replacements in council homes.
- **February 2023** (scrutiny 2 Feb): a pilot whole-house approach to decarbonising council homes;
  plus a Cabinet amendment on 8 February creating a £0.100m "Love Norwich" fund for ward
  priorities, split evenly between General Fund and HRA capital receipts.
- **February 2024** (scrutiny 1 Feb): Cabinet on 7 February added £1.1m to the play area
  improvement programme over three years, £0.3m for the Eaton Park changing pavilions, and £0.85m
  for Sloughbottom Park.
- **February 2026**: the Labour group added £313,000 of items to the substantive motion —
  enforcement against fly-tipping and graffiti, communal-area improvements, community clean-ups
  and political assistant provision — funded from contingency; the Green and Liberal Democrat
  groups tabled costed amendments of their own including a £65,000 ward-level community cohesion
  fund (£5,000 per ward) and £45,000 for tree planting.

Sources: `raw/budget/BudgetCouncil-2022.pdf` §B, `BudgetCouncil-2023.pdf` §§B–C,
`BudgetCouncil-2024.pdf` recs. xxiii–xxv and paras 1–2,
`raw/cmis/2026-02-24_Council_updated-annex-c-labour-group-and-opposition-proposed-budget-amendments-v3.pdf`.

For the CouncilLens linkage tiers: the 2023/24 consultation found the top-rated services were
housing, waste and recycling, and parks and open spaces, and the February 2024 amendments all went
to parks and play. That is 🟡 **possible** at most. The Cabinet papers do not cite the consultation
as the reason, so we must not say they do.

## 5. What I could not find or verify

1. **Whether the savings promised each February were actually delivered by March.** The outturn
   reports explain service variances but do not track savings delivery as a single figure, so the
   most obvious accountability question in section 3.6 cannot be answered from published papers.
   The only explicit reference found is a £0.005m shortfall against the NCSL savings target in
   2024/25. Worth an FOI or a direct question to the council. Note this is about savings
   *delivery*: the council does explain, in the February reports, why the savings it *identified*
   fell short of target in 2020/21, 2022/23 and 2023/24, and those explanations are now quoted in
   3.6.
2. **Local Government Finance Settlement Core Spending Power tables**, per year. Without these I
   cannot say cleanly how much Norwich's government funding has risen or fallen in real terms
   since 2019/20, which is the single most-asked question about council finances. This is the
   biggest remaining hole.
3. **The 2026/27 funding shift, properly quantified.** On the council's own budget papers RSG
   rises from £0.589m to £5.100m while retained business rates fall from £8.271m to £6.600m, a net
   gain of about £2.8m. On MHCLG's returns, which is the only basis on which other councils can be
   compared, the same two lines give a net gain of about £3.4m against Cambridge losing about £6m.
   Quote one basis or the other, never both without saying so. It looks like the settlement redistributing towards more
   deprived areas, which the council anticipated in February 2025. It needs the settlement tables
   to state as fact rather than inference.
4. **Council tax levels before 2025/26 for the comparators.** MHCLG publishes the per-authority
   Band D series back many years; only the 2025-26/2026-27 workbook was retrieved. The Norwich
   series in table 3.1 comes from the council's own reports, which is sound, but the peer
   comparison covers two years only.
5. **The full text of EY's Audit Results Report and Auditor's Annual Report.** I have the audit
   certificates, the covering report to Audit Committee of 7 July 2026, the value-for-money
   interim report covering 2021/22 and 2022/23, and the council's own summary of EY's findings in
   the Annual Governance Statement — which is where the 2024/25 weaknesses are quoted from. The
   full appendices were not among the committee attachments retrieved. Note also that paragraph 6
   of the July 2026 covering report is internally contradictory: it refers to "a summary of the
   two weaknesses" and then says there are "no significant weaknesses identified in respect of
   the Council's financial and governance arrangements". I have relied on the audit certificate
   and the Annual Governance Statement, which are unambiguous.
6. **Oflog metrics are gone for good.** The Office for Local Government was dissolved on
   16 December 2024; oflog.data.gov.uk redirects to a closure notice. Its standardised metrics
   (reserves as a percentage of net revenue expenditure, debt servicing ratios, council tax
   collection rates) would have been the cleanest peer comparison available. I rebuilt what I
   could from MHCLG's raw returns, but a collection-rate comparison is still missing, and I did
   not check whether the National Archives holds a usable snapshot.
7. **The final cost of the Lion Homes wind-up.** £6.15m of loans impaired to nil, the shareholding
   written down, and a £4m loan impairment back in 2019/20. Whether those overlap, and what the
   members' voluntary liquidation eventually returns once the council takes back the assets, is
   not in any published document yet. The outcome of the whistleblowing investigation is not
   public either. Do not publish a "cost to taxpayers" figure: none exists.
8. **Whether any consultation ever changed a budget figure.** No evidence found that one did, but
   absence of evidence is not proof, and in six of the seven years at least half of respondents
   backed the proposal anyway, so there was nothing for it to change. Present it as "we could not find any",
   never as "it never has".
9. **Norwich City Services Ltd**, the remaining wholly owned company. Loans outstanding fell from
   £2.22m to £1.68m during 2024/25 and its accounts are audited separately by Shaw & Co. Its job
   evaluation exercise contributed to overspends in 2025/26. I have not examined its position.
10. **Anglia Square and the Aviva joint investment partnership.** £11.059m spent in 2025/26, fully
    funded by Homes England grant, inside a £350m scheme with a £34m Homes England grant and up to
    1,100 homes. The council's exposure and the partnership's terms deserve their own piece of work.
11. **Two MHCLG outturn years disagree with the audited accounts** (see 3.3): the 2022/23 return
    says £10.336m unallocated where the accounts say £8.252m, and the 2023/24 return records nil
    unallocated and £23.113m earmarked against £8.249m and £21.683m. The discrepancy itself is
    worth reporting on the site: two official sources, two answers. (v1 also claimed the 2020/21
    and 2023/24 Norwich outturn rows were missing from the extract. They are present, with full
    reserves columns; that claim is withdrawn.)

### The four year-on-year series

**Series A — what it costs you, and what the council spends.**
Band D city share and net revenue budget requirement, 2019/20 to 2026/27, on table 3.1. Both rise
gently. The interesting shape: every single rise has been 1.99% or 2.99%, just under the referendum
threshold, with the city share up 19.4% over seven years. If you want to set that against inflation,
save the ONS series first and cite it; do not assert the comparison from memory. Use the corrected
2024/25 figure, not the published recommendation, and footnote why.

**Series B — planned against actual, revenue and capital side by side.**
Two lines, five years, from tables 3.4 and 3.5. Plot revenue as the cash variance rather than the
percentage, because the council changed the denominator partway through the run and the percentages
are not comparable. Revenue tracks plan closely in four of five years and by 6.23% in 2023/24;
capital does not get near it in any year. Putting both on one chart is the single most useful thing this topic
could do, because "the council can't manage money" and "the council builds what it says it will"
are two different claims with two different answers, and the chart gives both.

**Series C — reserves, with the pandemic distortion labelled on the chart.**
General Fund working balance and earmarked reserves at 31 March, 2019 to 2026, from table 3.3,
with the prudent minimum drawn as a floor line and an explicit annotation on the 2021 spike
explaining the section 31 grant timing. Without that annotation the chart tells a false story
about money appearing and vanishing.

**Series D — where the balancing money comes from.**
Corporate Financing variance against service directorates' variance, 2021/22 to 2025/26, from
table 3.4. This is the chart that carries finding 1, and as far as I can tell nobody has published
it. It shows the crossover in 2023/24, when the service directorates moved into overspend and the
central pot began covering them. Label the components honestly in the caption: interest income is a
named and growing part of the Corporate Financing underspend, not the whole of it, and in 2023/24 a
reserve drawdown that was not needed and a change to the minimum revenue provision calculation were
both larger than the interest.

### Framing notes for whoever writes the page

Say early and plainly that the Housing Revenue Account is separate and cannot be spent on bins.
Give the housing numbers their own block rather than folding them into a total.

Resist the two easy narratives. "Reserves have halved since 2019" is technically true of the
working balance (£11.653m to £8.249m) and thoroughly misleading, because the fall was a planned,
published use of reserves and the balance has been stable for four years above a minimum the
council's own finance officer sets and publishes. "The council is sitting on £198m of reserves" is
also technically true, of total usable reserves, and worse, because nearly all of it is
ring-fenced housing money and the Major Repairs Reserve.

Show the section 5 items as gaps rather than as silence. The savings-delivery question matters
most: it is the one an accountable council ought to be able to answer, and currently cannot be shown
to. Keep it separate from savings identification, where the council does explain itself.

Two more traps this version had to be pulled out of, both worth remembering when the page is
written. Where a council changes the basis of a series midway (the variance denominator, the
commercial income ratio, the restated reserves balance), a chart that hides the change is worse than
no chart. And where a table's first column is an estimate for the year before the document's title
year, as in the treasury strategies, the label has to follow the column and not the cover. Getting
that wrong turned a rising borrowing requirement into a flat one in v1.

And flag the thing that overshadows everything after 2026/27: Local Government Reorganisation may
abolish this council entirely, and it has set aside £1m and created a Readiness Fund to prepare.
Every medium-term forecast in these documents carries that caveat, attached by the council itself.
