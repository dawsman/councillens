# Who decides what

Ask most people who runs their local services and they say "the council". In much of England there
are two councils, neither in charge of the other, and they do completely different jobs.

Norfolk is one of those places. Norwich City Council and Norfolk County Council both cover the same
streets. They are separate organisations with separate budgets, separate staff and separate
elections.

## The split, in Norfolk

**Norwich City Council** does the things you meet day to day:

- Council housing. Norwich owns and manages 14,211 homes, which is unusual: many district councils
  sold theirs decades ago.
- Bin collections, parks, street cleaning.
- Planning applications.
- Licensing: who may sell alcohol, put on live music, or serve hot food late at night.
- Environmental health, benefits admin, homelessness.

**Norfolk County Council** does the expensive, less visible things:

- Roads and potholes.
- Schools and libraries.
- Social care for children and adults, which eats most of its budget.
- Waste disposal, once the city council has collected it.

So a pothole complaint to Norwich City Council goes nowhere. Neither does a request to the county
about a noisy pub.

## And the bill?

One bill, three organisations taking a share. The city council collects it all and passes most of it
on. Of every pound of council tax paid in Norwich, the city keeps about 13p. Norfolk County Council
takes 73p. The Police and Crime Commissioner takes 14p.

That is worth sitting with. The council whose name is on the envelope keeps roughly an eighth of the
money. When a Norwich resident is angry about the size of their bill, most of that anger is aimed at
the wrong building.

Each of the three sets its own rise. In 2024/25 Norwich chose 2.99%, Norfolk chose 4.99% including a
social care charge, and the police chose 4.28%.

## Why it is set up this way

History, mostly. The two-tier system dates from a reorganisation in 1974, and it has been argued
about ever since. Supporters say a district council knows its own patch. Critics say two sets of
councillors, two chief executives and two finance teams cost more than one.

That argument is live right now. Local Government Reorganisation is a national programme that may
abolish councils like Norwich and fold them into larger unitary authorities. Norwich set aside £1m
in a "Readiness Fund" for 2026/27 to prepare. Every long-range forecast in its budget papers carries
that caveat, written in by the council itself.

## How to work out who owns your problem

Two shortcuts.

The first: if the service comes to your door or your street corner, it is usually the district. If
it involves a professional qualification, a vehicle fleet or a building full of specialists, it is
usually the county.

The second, and better one: type your postcode into gov.uk's "find your local council". It tells you
which councils cover you and what each does. Do that before you write to anyone.
