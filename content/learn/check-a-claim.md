# How to check a claim against a source

Someone tells you the council wasted millions, or saved millions, or ignored everyone who
responded. Here is how to find out, in about fifteen minutes, without trusting anyone.

## 1. Turn the claim into a question with a yes or no answer

"The council is wasting money" cannot be checked. Nothing in the world would settle it.

"The council spent more than it budgeted on services in 2025/26" can be checked. So can "the
council's own auditors criticised how it oversaw its housing company". Sharpen the claim until a
document could prove it wrong.

## 2. Work out which document would hold the answer

Match the question to the paper trail:

- What was planned? The budget report, published each February.
- What was actually spent? The outturn report, usually June or July.
- What was decided, and by whom? The minutes and the resolution.
- What did people say? The consultation report.
- Is the council managing its money properly? The external auditor's report.

## 3. Find it on the council's own website

Go to the council's site rather than a search engine, and look for "committees", "meetings" or
"transparency". Norwich keeps its accounts and budget papers under a transparency and accountability
section.

Prefer the original over anyone's write-up, including ours. A press release is written to persuade.
A committee report is written to inform councillors who can ask awkward questions.

## 4. Read around the number

This is where most claims fall apart, and it takes practice.

A real example. Norwich's published figure for how much of its core funding comes from commercial
property fell from 16% to 4% in three years. That looks like a collapse. Read the notes and two
things emerge: the council added a notional borrowing cost into the later calculation, so the years
are not struck on the same basis, and the figure it divides by grew by more than a third. The
decline is real. It is not as steep as the two numbers suggest.

Ask, every time: same definition? Same time period? Same thing being divided by the same thing?

## 5. Quote it whole, or not at all

Auditors at Norwich wrote that "there is no indication that adequate oversight arrangements for
subsidiaries have ever existed". Strong stuff. But the sentence actually opens "Based on the
evidence available", and dropping those four words turns a hedged professional finding into a flat
accusation.

Cutting a quote to make it hit harder is the most common way honest people mislead. Copy the whole
sentence, and give the page number.

## What to do when the record is silent

Sometimes the document you need does not exist. Norwich officers told a committee in March 2026 that
a cumulative impact assessment would run alongside the licensing consultation. We looked. Nothing
has been published.

The honest conclusion is "we cannot tell", not "so it never happened" and not "so they are hiding
it". Silence in the record is a finding of its own, and worth reporting as exactly that.

## The habit

Claim, question, document, context, whole quote. Do it three or four times and it stops feeling like
work. It is also, more or less, the entire method behind this website.
