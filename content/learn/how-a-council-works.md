# How a council works

A council is two things bolted together. There are the people you elect, and the people it employs.
Knowing which is which explains almost everything else.

## The people you elect

Councillors are elected to represent a ward, usually for four years. Norwich has 39 of them. Most
have other jobs and are paid an allowance rather than a salary. They do three things: vote on
policy, sit on committees, and take up problems for people who live in their ward.

Whichever party or group has the most councillors normally runs the council and picks a **Cabinet**,
a small team of senior councillors who each look after a subject. Cabinet takes most decisions.

## The people it employs

Officers are staff. They research the options, write the reports councillors read, and then carry
out whatever gets decided. They are politically neutral by law and stay in post when control of the
council changes hands.

That split matters when you read a council report. The recommendation at the top is advice from
officers. The decision belongs to councillors, and they are free to ignore it.

## How something actually gets decided

Roughly this, every time:

1. **A problem or a legal deadline appears.** Licensing policies, for instance, must be remade at
   least every five years. Norwich approved its current one in December 2021, which set a clock
   running.
2. **Officers write a report** setting out options and recommending one.
3. **The report goes on a published agenda**, usually a week or so before the meeting. Anyone can
   read it. Almost nobody does.
4. **A committee or Cabinet discusses it in public.** Sometimes a scrutiny committee looks first and
   suggests changes.
5. **A vote happens** and the decision is written down as a resolution, in fixed wording.
6. **The minutes record it**, and are signed off at the next meeting.

Here it is with real dates. Norwich's licensing committee met on 5 March 2026, agreed a draft policy
and asked for a public consultation. Cabinet approved that on 11 March. The consultation ran from
18 May to 9 August and drew 67 responses. The meeting where those responses get reported was set
for 17 September 2026. Full Council votes on the final policy later.

Five months from draft to closing date. That pace is normal, and it is why "the council is doing
nothing" is so often wrong and so hard to disprove.

## Where you can get in

More places than most people think.

- **Read the agenda before the meeting**, not the news report after it.
- **Turn up.** Council meetings are public and many are webcast.
- **Ask a public question.** Most councils allow them, with notice, and the answer is minuted.
- **Respond to a consultation.** It takes ten minutes and the response count is often in the dozens,
  so one reply is worth more than you would guess.
- **Email your councillor.** Free, and often faster than a complaints form.

## The bit that trips people up

A council can only do what the law lets it do. It cannot fix a pothole in a two-tier area, or run a
school, or send more police, however many residents ask. The next page is about that.
