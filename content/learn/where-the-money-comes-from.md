# Where the money comes from, and where it goes

Council money looks complicated because it is kept in separate pots on purpose. Learn the pots and
the rest follows.

## Three sources of income

**Council tax.** Every home pays, based on a band from A to H set by what the property would have
sold for in April 1991. Yes, 1991. Nobody has revalued since. Norwich's own share of a middle-band
bill was £315.26 in 2026/27.

**Business rates.** A tax on shops, offices, warehouses. The council keeps a share and passes the
rest up, and the share is set nationally, so this income can swing without anything changing locally.
Norwich budgeted for £8.271m in 2025/26 and £6.600m the year after.

**Government grant.** Money from central government, some tied to a named job and some, like Revenue
Support Grant, free to spend. Norwich's Revenue Support Grant went from £0.589m to £5.100m in a
single year. Before you read that as a windfall, most of it was funding shifted across from business
rates rather than new money. The two lines have to be read together.

## Two pots

**The General Fund** pays for everyday services: bins, parks, planning, benefits admin, licensing.
Norwich's net General Fund budget for 2026/27 was £24.250m.

**The Housing Revenue Account** pays for council housing, and is fenced off by law. Rent money can
only be spent on housing. General Fund money cannot be used to prop it up, and housing money cannot
be raided to plug a hole in bin collections.

Mixing these two up is the commonest way to get a council's finances wrong. When a council says it
cannot afford something, the first question is always: out of which pot?

## Day-to-day against building work

One more split, and it catches people out.

**Revenue** spending is running costs. Wages, fuel, contracts, small repairs.

**Capital** spending buys things that last. Homes, roofs, vehicles, play equipment. Councils can
borrow for capital but broadly not for revenue, which is why the two are budgeted and reported
separately.

Capital plans slip constantly. Norwich approved £342.9m of capital work over the five years to
2025/26 and spent £218.6m of it. That is 64%. In 2025/26 alone, £38.3m of approved work did not
happen. The money is not usually lost, it rolls forward, but a council that has never once hit its
building programme is telling you something about how it plans.

## Where it goes

Roughly, for a district council like Norwich: housing (its own pot), waste and street cleaning,
parks and leisure, planning and regulation, benefits administration, homelessness, plus the cost of
running the organisation itself.

What you will not find is social care, schools or roads. Those belong to the county, and they are
where the really large sums sit.

## The number to look for

Any council can produce a budget that balances on paper. The figure that tells you whether it holds
is the **outturn**: what was actually spent, reported after the year ends. Norwich takes its outturn
report to Cabinet each June or July.
