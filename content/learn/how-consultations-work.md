# How consultations work, and how to respond

A consultation is a council formally asking what people think, usually through a survey with a
closing date. Sometimes the law requires one. Often the council chooses to run one anyway.

It is not a vote, and treating it as one leads to a lot of wasted anger.

## What it can and cannot do

A council must genuinely consider the answers. It does not have to follow them. It can decide
against the majority view as long as it can show it took the responses into account, and there are
usually good reasons why: a legal duty to balance the budget, a cap it cannot exceed, a duty to
people who did not respond.

Norwich has consulted on its council tax rise every year since 2019. For six years running, at least
half of respondents backed the rise. Then in 2026/27 the balance flipped: 28% supported, 48%
opposed, 25% were neutral, out of 899 responses. The council adopted 2.99% anyway.

You can call that ignoring the public. You can also note that 2.99% was the most it could charge
without holding a referendum, and that it published the unflattering result in full rather than
burying it. Both readings use the same document.

## How small the numbers usually are

This is the part that changes how people behave.

Norwich's housing consultation in late 2025 drew **72 responses**, on rules affecting around 4,000
people on the waiting list. Its licensing consultation, running twelve weeks to August 2026, drew
**67**.

Sixty-seven people, in a city of well over a hundred thousand. One thoughtful response is a meaningful share of the
evidence a committee sees, and that is not an exaggeration to make you feel better.

## Writing a response that gets used

Officers have to summarise every response for councillors. Make yours easy to summarise.

- **Answer the question asked.** A response about parking, sent to a licensing consultation, gets
  logged and set aside.
- **Say which proposal you mean**, by its number or its heading. Consultations usually cover several
  changes at once, and Norwich's housing one covered five.
- **Give a reason, not just a view.** "I disagree" is one tick. "I disagree, because a two-year
  residence rule would exclude people leaving care" is a point an officer has to write up.
- **Say what you would do instead.** Alternatives get quoted. Objections get counted.
- **Be specific about who is affected.** Groups protected by the Equality Act carry particular
  weight, because the council has a legal duty to consider them.
- **Keep it short.** Half a page beats five.

## What to check afterwards

Two things.

**Did the council publish the results?** It should. Norwich published a consultation report for the
homelessness strategy but not for the housing allocations half of the same survey, which is why this
site logs that as a gap.

**Did anything change?** Look for a "you said, we did" section, or compare the draft with the version
finally adopted. After the 2025 housing consultation, Norwich changed how the rules treat rent
arrears caused by financial abuse, and said in writing that the consultation was the reason. That
one gets a green label on this site. Most do not.
