# How to read a council budget in ten minutes

A council budget report runs to hundreds of pages. You need about six of them. Here is the order I
would read them in, using Norwich as the worked example.

Set a timer if you like. Ten minutes is genuinely enough for a first pass.

## 1. Find the headline number (1 minute)

Go to the recommendations at the front. There will be a line naming the net revenue budget
requirement and the council tax rise. For Norwich in 2026/27: £24.250m, and a rise of 2.99%.

Write both down. Everything else is detail hanging off those two.

## 2. Check the rise against the referendum limit (30 seconds)

Councils cannot raise council tax above a limit set by government without holding a referendum,
which nobody wants to pay for. That limit has recently sat around 3%.

Norwich has raised its share by 1.99% or 2.99% every year since 2019/20. Never higher. That pattern
is common across England, and it tells you the ceiling is doing the deciding as much as the council
is.

## 3. Find the funding table (2 minutes)

Look for an appendix listing council tax, retained business rates and government grant. Compare this
year's column with last year's.

In Norwich's 2026/27 table, grant rose by £4.5m while business rates fell by £1.7m. A quick reader
sees a windfall. A careful one sees money moved between two lines, and a net gain of roughly £2.8m.
Always read the funding lines as a set.

## 4. Go to the savings list (2 minutes)

Somewhere there is a table of savings, each with a value and a service attached. This is where a
budget stops being abstract. Read the descriptions, not just the totals, and note anything described
as a "review", a "restructure" or "increased income", because those often mean charging you more or
employing fewer people.

## 5. Find the section 151 statement (1 minute)

By law the council's chief finance officer must say in writing whether the budget is sound and the
reserves are big enough. It is usually a short section near the back and it is written carefully.
Careful language is the point. When this officer expresses doubt, it matters enormously.

## 6. Compare last year's budget with last year's outturn (3 minutes)

This is the step almost nobody does, and it is the one that pays.

Pull up the outturn report from the previous summer. Norwich's 2025/26 outturn shows services
overspent by £2.282m, while the council as a whole came in about 1% under budget, because a central
financing line underspent by £2.992m.

Both facts are true at once, and neither is a scandal on its own. But a council whose services
overspend three years running while a central line quietly rescues them is running a different
system from the one its budget describes. You only see that by putting the two documents side by
side.

## What you now know

The headline, whether the rise hit the ceiling, where the money comes from, what is being cut, what
the finance officer thinks, and whether last year's plan survived contact with reality.

That is more than most people who write about council budgets bother to find out.
